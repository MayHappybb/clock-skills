The display is the Luat Air101-LCD: a 0.96" IPS colour LCD, 160 x 80 pixels
(21.7 x 10.8 mm), RGB565, ST7735S on SPI, white LED backlight. It is a real
colour pixel canvas: scenes, characters, real typography and flat graphic
art are all possible. The owner asked for "all your creativity": a theme,
fluent and smooth motion (both the constant life and the transitions), and
interesting fonts that suit the theme. And 60 fps: they want the high
refresh rate because it looks smoother.

THE OWNER'S REQUEST, VERBATIM: "Pick surprising themes where the numerals
ARE the theme's characters or material. The digits themselves are alive
every second, on a beat, not only at the turn. Minute and hour transitions
are smooth and happen through the theme's own action. Each face gets its
own font, drawn for its theme. The time must read correctly in every
frame." Style: full colour, flat 2D (below).

THIS ROUND: FULL COLOUR, FLAT 2D, A FEW RESTRAINED EFFECTS. The last three
rounds were strict 1-bit; this one is back to colour. The owner likes flat
2D graphic themes most on this panel: xray best of all (honest false-colour
imaging, translucent overlapping objects, no fake depth), then escher
(Metamorphosis: a woodcut tiling, flat two-tone creatures with keylines),
illuminated (flat lapis, vermilion and gold, white filigree) and lascaux
(flat pigment on a rock wall). Their reason: "the screen has low
resolution; if you add 3D effects like shading, round animals and so on,
it doesn't express well." And flat must not mean plain: "that would be too
boring". So:
- Work in a flat graphic medium and obey its rules: flat fills from a
  small deliberate palette, crisp keylines or none, silhouettes that read,
  and the medium's own flat texture (halftone, hatching, spray grain, print
  misregistration, woodblock grain, scanlines, tile grout).
- Add two or three restrained effects that belong to the medium: a glint
  travelling along a surface, a slow light sweep, a small glow, a shimmer,
  a wave rolling through repeated elements, sparkle on a few pixels, a
  colour front. Alive and precious, never busy.
- No 3D modelling: no shading ramps that fake volume, no specular blobs
  that make a shape round, bevels, ambient occlusion, soft drop shadows,
  inflated shapes, perspective scenes or depth-of-field. A flat cast
  shadow or an offset print layer is fine if the medium has it.
- Hard pixels are the default; AA only where a big flat shape needs a
  clean edge or a thin line glides sub-pixel. Design at the pixel.
- Night: design the night palette on purpose (dim through
  ctx.lcd.backlight and/or a deliberate darker palette); render 02:47:30 at
  --scale 4 and check every digit still reads.

THE TIME IS ALIVE (the main request). In every earlier colour round the
numerals sat still between minute turns while a few small things moved
around them, and the wow lived only in the turn. The owner's verdict on
the round that fixed this (1-bit, round 9): "very amazing", above all
rubberhose, where the four numerals are 1930s cartoon characters dancing
on the beat. What worked: the numerals ARE the theme's characters or
material, they move every second on a beat, and the turn happens through
the theme's own action. So the TIME ITSELF is in visible motion every
second of the minute, at rest, not only at :59-:01:
- The numerals are made of, or carried by, something that moves: they
  bounce, dance, flow, pulse, wave, march, are worked and reworked, are
  crossed by travelling fronts, ripples or patterns, or are characters
  that act. A viewer glancing at any second of the minute sees the digits
  alive.
- The world around them is busy too, with big moves (not only specks), but
  calmer than the digits.
- A rhythm: a beat once a second (or twice) on top of continuous motion,
  plus a bigger flourish every ~10-15 s, so the minute has a structure.
- Readability still wins: at EVERY frame the time reads correctly at
  --scale 4. Motion that bends, shakes or animates a digit must never make
  it read as another digit (no rotation that mirrors or narrows it, no
  limb or part that closes a counter or opens a bowl). Keep the numerals'
  silhouette steady enough to read, their surface and edges alive.
- Measure it: from the skill directory run
    node ../../../prompts/round9/alive.mjs pachinko
  It builds 1 s of consecutive frames at three rest moments and reports
  "frame" (% of the glass changing each frame), "second" (% changing within
  a second) and "ink" (% of the non-ground pixels in the central area that
  change within a second; it takes the commonest colour as ground, so in
  colour it is a rough proxy). The old colour favourites, whose digits sit
  still: xray second 8.6 ink 10.9, escher 90.8/26.2, illuminated 57.6/4.9,
  lascaux 98.8/5.3. The loved 1-bit round 9: ink 73-99, second 35-77.
  Targets: second at least ~30% and ink at least ~40% at every rest
  moment, with the time still reading. The number is not the judge: say
  in the report what the digits themselves do each second.
- Dynamic, fancy effects at the turn and the hour: a flowing minute turn,
  a real wow at the hour.
- Read sim/designs/xray.js and escher.js (and their helpers) first, and
  rubberhose.js for the beat. Note how xray derives every pixel from flat
  material maps through a palette.

Starting idea. It is short on purpose: invent the details yourself (the
palette, the font, the life at rest, the beat, the turn, the hour). Push
past it, or replace it with something stronger in its world:
A Showa-era pachinko playfield, flat printed board, brass pins. Steel balls (flat discs with one glint) rain from the top, ricochet through the pin field and pile into pockets shaped as the numerals, so each digit is a heap of jostling balls; the jackpot lights chase on every beat. Minute turn: the old pocket's gate opens and its balls pour out while the new digit fills after :00. Hour: fever mode, the time still reading.

GOAL: a stranger looks at it and says "wait, that's a clock?" Creativity
comes first. A competent, pretty, conventional face is a fail.

REQUIREMENTS
1. Concept: the time is expressed by the theme's own mechanism. Test: if
   swapping your digits for a plain font still works, the digits are not
   part of the idea yet.
2. Letterforms: draw your own, made of or carried by the theme's material
   or characters, designed for this pixel grid. The kit's fonts
   (strokeDigit, segDigit, dotText, 3x5, 5x7) are not allowed for the time,
   not even as a skeleton with texture painted on. Design all ten digits as
   a set and make them beautiful at this resolution, every edge designed.
   Small secondary type (a date, a label), if any, is in the same world's
   style.
3. Use the canvas: a 2:1 strip, the whole 160x80, the backlight. Never the
   default of four equal digits in a row with decoration around them.
4. Always alive at 60 fps: smooth motion that belongs to the theme, every
   second. At most ~1 px per frame per moving thing reads as a glide; the
   crystal's ~15 ms response is about one 60 fps frame, so fast
   high-contrast motion smears (renders show it; that is the real look).
5. Minute turn: the old digit physically becomes the new one through the
   theme's process, 0.8-3 s, eased, no pops or cuts. The time is never wrong
   mid-transition (empty or half-drawn is fine; a different number is not).
   The hour gets a bigger, distinct show, and the time stays readable
   through it (blank for more than ~1 s is a fail).
6. Colour: a small flat palette chosen for the theme; say why in the header
   comment. The time is the strongest contrast on the glass, the ground
   calmer. It is an LCD: black is a dark grey that glows a little.
7. 60 fps on a small MCU: fps: 60 means a 16.7 ms budget. Keep the rough C
   estimate for esp32c3 (no FPU, the weakest profile) at most ~14 ms in the
   worst frame of check.mjs, and the mean well under. Per-pixel callbacks
   (paint, fillWhere, fillSDF) and gradients over big areas are what blow
   it: precompute sprites, masks or distance fields per minute and look them
   up; kit.sin/cos, not Math.sin, in hot loops. No per-pixel allocation or
   closures in hot loops; keep hot objects monomorphic. Check tearing for
   fast horizontal motion of tall shapes (render --tear).
8. Real size: the whole face is 21.7 x 10.8 mm seen from arm's length.
   Detail finer than ~2 px vanishes. Judge legibility at --scale 4 (about
   true size) as well as zoomed.
9. Technical:
   - frame() is a pure function of time: closed form, or deterministic
     caches keyed by the minute. If you must simulate, simulate from the
     start of the current minute inside frame, warmup <= 2 s of work.
   - `node tools/check.mjs --design pachinko` passes (it takes ~40 s at 60 fps),
     and `node ../../../prompts/round9/alive.mjs pachinko` meets the targets.
   - RAM: keep working buffers at most ~150 KB beyond the 25 KB frame
     buffer, and state the peak in the report.
   - Work done outside the main canvas (a once-a-minute redraw into your
     own buffer) is invisible to the estimate: keep it small or spread it
     over several frames, and state its rough cost in the report.
   - The kit's colour helpers (mix, dither565, ...) are billed as Math
     calls per use; in per-pixel loops use your own integer arithmetic.
   - Flash: precomputed tables and sprites <= ~256 KB on the device
     (RGB565 = 2 bytes a pixel); state the total in the report.
   - No hidden extra features.

PROCESS
1. Pitch three very different concepts in your theme's world. Pick the
   strongest, judging harshly.
2. Digits first. Render every digit in every slot:
   node tools/render.mjs pachinko --clocks 00:00:30,11:11:30,22:22:30,03:33:30,04:44:30,05:55:30,16:06:30,17:17:30,18:18:30,19:39:30 --px 3 --cols 2 --out out/pachinko-digits.png
   and read each digit in the text maps. Iterate until each digit is
   beautiful and unmistakable.
3. Build, then render these (and check them through text maps):
   - out/pachinko.png (and --scale 4 --out out/pachinko-true.png)
   - out/pachinko-turn.png: --clock 10:09:58 --frames 12 --duration 4 --sheet --px 3 --cols 3
   - out/pachinko-hour.png: the same at --clock 10:59:58, and once at 23:59:58
   - out/pachinko-day.png: --clocks 02:47:30,07:15:30,12:00:30,18:05:30 --px 3
   - out/pachinko.gif: --clock 10:09:57 --frames 150 --duration 3 --gif --px 4
     (50 fps is the GIF limit), and out/pachinko-hour.gif at 10:59:57
   Contact sheets hide pops: map dense steps (every ~0.1 s) through each
   transition before calling it smooth and correct.
4. Critique it in the owner's voice. Is the font, layout, motion or
   transition ordinary? Do the digits themselves move every second? Is
   there a wow moment? Fix the weakest part and re-check. At least two
   rounds.
5. Report:
   - the idea in one sentence
   - what the digits do each second at rest, the flourish, the turn, the
     hour
   - render paths
   - the check.mjs result (incl. esp32c3 max/mean estimate), the alive.mjs
     result, flash and RAM totals
   - the honest weakest point

LESSONS FROM EARLIER ROUNDS (these are the fixes I had to send back)
- At rest the face still read as four plain digits with a themed
  background: make the time belong to the theme, carried by its objects,
  characters or material.
- The strongest stills put the time on a big, bold physical object of the
  theme, with the world alive around it.
- Characters below ~12 px read as blobs: draw heroes bold and simple
  (outlined, a few flat colours, clear eyes), with their defining
  silhouette parts big (horns, ears, tails, wings), and check at --scale 4.
- Anything else that looks like a time, or a numeral-sized ring or stroke
  beside the digits, competes with the time. Loose actors near the digits
  read as extra digits ("i47"): keep them away or clearly smaller/dimmer.
- Background made of the same stuff and saturation as the digits competes
  with them: calm the ground, give numerals a keyline or a 3-4 px moat.
- Digits that read as LETTERS: a peaked 4 read "A", 4 read "U", 9 read "a"
  in a squat slot, 7/T, 1/I. A 6 or 0 whose counter fills reads as 8;
  snapping digits to a coarse grid of theme units filled counters (6 -> 8,
  09 -> B8). Draw each digit as a clean shape with open counters first,
  then let the material fill or clip to it.
- A limb resting over a digit made 0 read as 6; an effect that changes over
  the minute closed a counter late (check :05, :30 and :55).
- A digit that rotates or turns while shown read as another (0 edge-on
  read 1).
- Partial digits read as other digits: a top-down wipe left an 8's lower
  loop (read 0); a 9 whose bowl came first read 0; a dotted 7 read "?"; a
  thinning 0 read "c"; a 1 removed before the 9 beside it made 10:19 read
  10:09. Remove every changing digit on the same schedule, evenly (by
  fraction of its own pixels, ranked once), or reveal the whole outline
  first, then fill. Removal must thin a digit or throw its material away,
  never close its open sides.
- Work changing digits concurrently; keep the unreadable window under ~1 s,
  and plan the midnight four-digit change first (blanks crept to ~1 s).
- Never move the whole time across a screen edge (an hour scroll showed
  "4·59" at 14:59:59).
- A shape dashing at ~8 px a frame smeared and tore: speed lines are fine,
  but keep the shape's own motion near 1-2 px/frame.
- Night failures: muddy colours on near-black, far digits vanishing.
- A face's worst frame and RAM were far over budget: measure them for real.
- Particles carrying the time: give each a home position precomputed per
  minute and derive its motion in closed form from time and index
  (kit.noise, kit.sin), so any frame can be built cold.
- Don't add the owner's avatar or any recurring mascot. Characters belong
  to the theme.
- Keep sim/designs/pachinko.js importable at all times: every tool loads
  index.js, which imports every design, so a half-written file breaks
  the other agents' renders too. If your renders fail because another
  design is mid-edit, wait a minute and retry; don't touch their file.
